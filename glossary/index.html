REFERENCE / DEFINITIONS

Core terminology

Terms used to describe wrestling characters, the performance’s fictional framework, and mistakes during execution.

Heel
A heel is a professional wrestling character portrayed as an antagonist. Heels commonly use rule-breaking, boastfulness, intimidation, or hostility to provoke audience disapproval and create opposition for a babyface. The role describes a character’s alignment in a story, not a wrestler’s real-life personality. Roman Reigns, for example, was presented as a dominant heel as the Tribal Chief beginning in 2020; his character’s reception and alignment have changed over time.
Babyface
A babyface, also called a face, is a protagonist or sympathetic competitor presented as someone the audience should support. Babyfaces are often portrayed as resilient, honorable, or opposed to a heel’s unfair conduct, although character traits and audience reactions vary. A wrestler who changes alignment from heel to babyface, or vice versa, has made a turn.
Kayfabe
Kayfabe is the presentation of professional wrestling’s characters, rivalries, and events as genuine within the performance’s fictional world. The term also describes maintaining that illusion outside the match. Historically, promotions often protected kayfabe more strictly; wider media access and public acknowledgment of wrestling’s scripted storytelling have made it more openly understood. Modern performers may still maintain character and narrative continuity, while interviews and broadcasts can discuss how the business works. Scripted outcomes do not make the athletic performance or risk of injury unreal.
Botch
A botch is an unintended error during a wrestling move, sequence, entrance, or other planned performance element. A botch can be a harmless timing or coordination mistake, but a failed move can also cause serious injury. The Shockmaster’s fall through a wall during his 1993 WCW debut at Starrcade is a famous entrance botch. Sid Vicious’s leg was broken after a botched leap from the top rope during his match at WCW Sin in 2001. These incidents illustrate different consequences; “botch” describes the execution failure, not necessarily the performer’s intent.
